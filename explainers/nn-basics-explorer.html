§1 · The idea

Rules learned from examples

Try to write a program that tells a T-shirt from a sneaker. "If the top half is wide, it's a T-shirt…" What about a T-shirt photographed sideways, or a crumpled one? Hand-written rules break almost immediately.

Machine learning (ML) flips the job around. Instead of writing the rule yourself, you show the computer thousands of examples with the right answers, and it works out a rule that fits them.

Normal programmingrules + data
→ answers
Machine learningdata + answers
→ rules
A model is a machine with knobs. Turn the knobs and its answers change. Training means turning the knobs until its answers match the examples, like tuning an old radio until the station comes in clearly.
Model: a formula that turns an input into an answer. Parameters (also called weights): the knobs, the numbers inside the formula that training is allowed to change. Training: the process of choosing those numbers.

The same three ingredients, every time

Everything on this page, up to the CNN you built, uses the same recipe. Each section is tagged with the ingredient it's about.

§2 · The simplest model model

A formula with two knobs

You hang weights on a spring and measure how far it stretches. Hooke's law says stretch grows in a straight line with force, but real measurements are noisy, so the 24 points below don't sit exactly on one line.

Our model is a straight line:

\hat y = \pw{w}\,\bl{x} + \pw{b}
\bl{x}: the input (force). \hat y ("y-hat"): the model's guess for the stretch. The hat always means "predicted". y without a hat is the true measured value.
\pw{w}: the slope, i.e. how much the guess rises when x goes up by 1.
\pw{b}: the intercept, i.e. the guess when x = 0.
The two knobs are \pw{w} and \pw{b}.
Worked example: \pw{w}=0.6,\ \pw{b}=0.6, force \bl{x}=2
\hat y = \pw{0.6}\times\bl{2} + \pw{0.6} = 1.2 + 0.6 = 1.8

A model is just a formula. Its knobs (here \pw{w} and \pw{b}) are the numbers training will choose. The inputs \bl{x} are given; you never choose those.
§3 · Measuring wrongness loss

One number for "how wrong"

To improve the knobs, we first need a score that says whether a setting is better or worse. That score is called the loss. Lower is better, and 0 means perfect.

Step 1: the error for one point

e_i = \hat y_i - y_i

The little i means "point number i". Positive e_i: the line is above the point (guessed too high). Negative: guessed too low.

Step 2: why not just add the errors?

Errors of +1 and -1 add to 0, which would say "perfect" for a line that misses both points. We need every miss to count as bad.

Step 3: square them, then average

\og{L} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} e_i^{\,2} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\pw{w}\,\bl{x_i} + \pw{b} - y_i\right)^2
\sum_{i=1}^{N} ("sigma") means "add this up for every point, from i=1 to i=N". N is the number of points, so dividing by N makes it an average. This loss is called the mean squared error (MSE).
Why squares? ① A square is never negative, so misses can't cancel. ② Big misses count much more than small ones: an error of 3 costs 9, but an error of 1 costs only 1. ③ A square is a smooth curve with no sharp corners, so we can take its slope, which §4 needs.

Notice that the loss depends only on the knobs: change \pw{w} or \pw{b} and you get a different number. So the loss is a function of the knobs, written \og{L}(\pw{w},\pw{b}). Finding the best knobs means finding where this function is lowest.

The loss is one number for how wrong the current knobs are. It only measures; it doesn't fix anything. MSE = the average of the squared misses.
§4 · Going downhill optimizer

Follow the slope down

We want the knobs where the loss is lowest. Trying every setting is impossible: your CNN has 10,330 knobs. We need a way to know which direction to turn each knob.

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the valley floor. You can't see it, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, and step again. The hillside is the loss, your position is the knob settings, and your height is the loss value.

One knob first

Freeze \pw{b} and look at the loss as you slide only \pw{w}. Because the loss is built from squares, the curve is a bowl.

The derivative \og{\tfrac{dL}{dw}} is the slope of that curve (the orange tangent line). Positive slope: the loss rises if \pw{w} goes up, so go down. Negative slope: go up. Zero slope: you're at the bottom.

That gives the whole rule, called gradient descent:

\pw{w}_{\text{new}} = \pw{w} - \eta\,\og{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}}
\eta ("eta") is the learning rate: the step size. The minus sign is what makes you go downhill. The curly \partial is just "d" for a function with several knobs: "the slope in the w direction, holding the other knobs still". Doing this for every knob, every step, is the optimizer's job.

Where does the slope formula come from?

Take the MSE and differentiate with respect to \pw{w}. One rule does all the work, the chain rule: the derivative of (\text{stuff})^2 is 2\cdot\text{stuff}\cdot\frac{d(\text{stuff})}{dw}.

\begin{aligned}\og{L} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \big(\underbrace{\pw{w}\bl{x_i} + \pw{b} - y_i}_{\text{stuff} \;=\; e_i}\big)^2\\[4pt] \og{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_i 2\,e_i\cdot\underbrace{\bl{x_i}}_{\frac{d\,\text{stuff}}{dw}} \;=\; \frac{2}{N}\sum_i e_i\,\bl{x_i}\\[4pt] \og{\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}} &= \frac{2}{N}\sum_i e_i\cdot 1 \;=\; \frac{2}{N}\sum_i e_i\end{aligned}
Read it in words. \og{\partial L/\partial w} is the average of (error × input). If the line is too low at the big-x points (negative errors where x is large), the sum is negative, so the update raises \pw{w}, making the line steeper. The formula is just "fix the misses, weighted by where they are".

Both knobs at once

With two knobs, the loss is a surface over the (\pw{w},\pw{b}) plane, like a valley on a map. The two slopes together form the gradient, an arrow \og{\nabla L} = \left(\og{\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}},\og{\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial b}}\right) that points straight uphill. Gradient descent steps against it.

§5 · Logistic regression modelloss

From "how much" to "which one"

Now each point belongs to a class: blue or red. Each point has two coordinates, \bl{x_1} and \bl{x_2}. We want the model to output the probability that a point is blue, a number between 0 and 1.

A straight-line formula can output 7 or −3, which aren't probabilities. So we build the model in two steps.

Step 1: a score z

z = \pw{w_1}\bl{x_1} + \pw{w_2}\bl{x_2} + \pw{b}

This is the same weighted sum as before, just with two inputs. z can be any number. Its sign says which side of a line the point is on:

The points where z = 0 form a straight line (an equation of the form a x_1 + c x_2 + d = 0). On one side z \gt 0, on the other z \lt 0, and |z| grows the further you are from the line.
In vector form, z = \pw{\mathbf w}\cdot\bl{\mathbf x} + \pw{b}, a dot product. \pw{\mathbf w} = (\pw{w_1}, \pw{w_2}) is the normal vector of the line z = 0: it points straight across the line, towards the side where z is positive. So changing \pw{\mathbf w} rotates the line, and changing \pw{b} slides it without rotating. The purple arrow in the plot below is \pw{\mathbf w}.

Step 2: squash the score into a probability

The sigmoid function, written \sigma ("sigma"), squashes any number into the range 0 to 1:

p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
z−4−2024
σ(z)0.020.120.500.880.98
Why this shape? Big negative z makes e^{-z} huge, so p \to 0: confident red. Big positive z makes e^{-z} \to 0, so p \to 1: confident blue. At z = 0, p = \tfrac{1}{1+1} = 0.5: can't tell. The curve is also symmetric, \sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z).
The decision boundary (where the model says 50/50) is where p = 0.5, which is where z = 0, which is a straight line. This model can only ever split the plane with a straight line.
Worked example: knobs \pw{w_1}=-0.8,\ \pw{w_2}=1.5,\ \pw{b}=1
Blue point (1, 1):
\begin{aligned} z &= (-0.8)(1) + (1.5)(1) + 1 = 1.7\\ p &= \sigma(1.7) = \bl{0.85}\end{aligned}
Red point (-1, -1):
\begin{aligned} z &= (-0.8)(-1) + (1.5)(-1) + 1 = 0.3\\ p &= \sigma(0.3) = \rd{0.57}\end{aligned}

The blue point is handled well. The red point gets p = 0.57, so the model leans "blue" for a red point. These knobs need training.

The loss: cross-entropy

For classification we score the probability the model gave to the true class:

\og{L} = -\ln\big(\text{probability given to the true class}\big)
prob. of true class10.90.50.10.01
loss −ln(p)00.110.692.304.61
Why −ln? Certain and right costs exactly 0. Unsure (0.5) costs a bit. Confident and wrong costs a lot, and the cost keeps growing without limit as the probability heads to 0. The model learns that bluffing is expensive. Written with the label y (1 for blue, 0 for red) it becomes \og{L} = -\big[y\ln p + (1-y)\ln(1-p)\big], which just picks -\ln p for blue points and -\ln(1-p) for red ones.

For the red point above: \og{L} = -\ln(1 - 0.57) = -\ln(0.43) = \og{0.85}. That's high, because the model was wrong.

The gradient: error × input, again

After the algebra (tap below if you want it), the slope comes out remarkably simple:

\og{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j}} = (p - y)\,\bl{x_j} \qquad \og{\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}} = p - y

(p - y) is the error: how far the probability was from the truth. It's the same "error × input" pattern as the line fit in §4.

Show the algebra (chain rule)
\begin{aligned}\frac{d\sigma}{dz} &= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\,\big(1-\sigma(z)\big) = p(1-p)\\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial p} &= -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1-y}{1-p}\\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{dp}{dz} = \Big(-\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1-y}{1-p}\Big)\,p(1-p)\\ &= -y(1-p) + (1-y)p = p - y\\[4pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_j} = (p-y)\,x_j\end{aligned}

The messy fractions cancel. That neat cancellation is a big reason sigmoid and cross-entropy are used together.

Worked: the red point (-1,-1), true label y = 0, p = 0.57
\og{\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}} = (0.57 - 0)\times(-1) = \og{-0.57}

It's negative, so gradient descent raises \pw{w_1}. For this point x_1 = -1, so a bigger w_1 makes w_1 x_1 more negative: z drops and the point's probability of being blue drops. That's exactly the fix it needs.

§6 · The neuron model

You just built a neuron

A neuron, the building block of every neural network, does exactly what §5 did. The name comes from brain cells, but in practice it's just this formula:

a = f\Big(\sum_j \pw{w_j}\,\bl{x_j} + \pw{b}\Big)
Multiply each input by its weight, add them up with the bias (that's z), then pass z through an activation function f. The result a is the neuron's output, its activation. §5 used f = \sigma.
Live: the point you picked in §5

Activation functions, and why their slope matters

Different bends get used. The dashed orange line is each one's slope f'(z). You'll see in §8 and §10 that this slope decides how much "blame" can flow back through the neuron during learning.

Neuron = weighted sum + bias, then a bend: a = f(\pw{\mathbf w}\cdot\bl{\mathbf x} + \pw{b}). The bend's slope f' controls how learning signals pass through it.
§7 · When one line isn't enough model

Bend the data, not the line

One neuron draws one straight boundary. What about a circle (blue inside, red outside), or XOR (blue where x_1 and x_2 have the same sign)? No straight line can separate those.

Why the new feature works

Give the neuron a third input, \bl{r^2} = \bl{x_1^2 + x_2^2}, the squared distance from the centre. Its score becomes

z = \pw{w_1}\bl{x_1} + \pw{w_2}\bl{x_2} + \pw{w_3}\,\bl{(x_1^2 + x_2^2)} + \pw{b}

Suppose training finds w_1 \approx w_2 \approx 0. The boundary z = 0 is then

\pw{w_3}(x_1^2 + x_2^2) + \pw{b} = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; x_1^2 + x_2^2 = -\frac{\pw{b}}{\pw{w_3}}

That's the equation of a circle with radius \sqrt{-b/w_3}. The neuron still makes a straight cut, but in the new feature (inside points have small r^2, outside points large). Back in the original picture, that straight cut is a circle.

Feature: an input number given to the model. Feature engineering: a person inventing new features (like x_1^2 + x_2^2 for circles, or x_1 x_2 for XOR) so a simple model can solve the problem. This is how much of classical ML works.
A straight cut in a clever feature = a curved boundary in the original picture. The catch: a human had to guess the right feature. For photos of clothes, nobody knows what to write down.
§8 · How a network learns features (1D) modeloptimizer

A ReLU neuron is a hinge

§7 needed a human to invent x_1^2 + x_2^2. A neural network builds its own features instead. To see how, start with one input \bl{x}, so everything fits on one graph.

One neuron = one hinge

h = \text{ReLU}(\pw{w}\,\bl{x} + \pw{b}) = \max(0,\ \pw{w}\,\bl{x} + \pw{b})

This is flat at 0 on one side and a straight ramp on the other, like a piece of card folded once. The fold, or hinge, is where the inside becomes zero:

\pw{w}\,x + \pw{b} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^{*} = -\frac{\pw{b}}{\pw{w}}

\pw{b}

\pw{b} slides the hinge left and right. \pw{w} sets the ramp's steepness, and its sign sets which way the ramp faces.

Add hinges to build any bendy shape

A layer of several neurons gives several hinges. The output neuron adds them up, each scaled by an output weight \pw{v_j}:

\hat y = \pw{v_1}h_1 + \pw{v_2}h_2 + \pw{v_3}h_3 + \pw{c}
Example: two hinges, both at 0, make a V
|x| = \text{ReLU}(x) + \text{ReLU}(-x)

Each extra hinge adds one corner. With enough hinges you can trace any curve as a chain of straight pieces.

Why the bend is essential. Remove ReLU and each "neuron" is just a straight line w_j x + b_j. Adding straight lines gives a straight line:
\pw{v_1}(\pw{w_1}x + \pw{b_1}) + \pw{v_2}(\pw{w_2}x + \pw{b_2}) + \pw{c} = (\pw{v_1}\pw{w_1} + \pw{v_2}\pw{w_2})\,x + (\dots)
No corners, however many you stack. The hinge is what makes extra neurons useful.

Watch training move the hinges

The target is a curved valley (white dots). The network has 3 hinge neurons, and all three start bunched on the right. Each coloured thin line is one neuron's contribution \pw{v_j}h_j, and the dashed lines mark the hinges. The yellow curve is their sum, the network's output. Loss: MSE, optimizer: plain gradient descent, as in §4.

Nobody told the hinges where to go. They spread out to where the curve bends, because that's what lowers the loss. Each hinge has become a feature: "how far past this point are you?". So how does gradient descent know which way to push each one?

The blame for one neuron, in four factors

Take one data point, with error e = \hat y - y and loss \og{L} = e^2. Follow the chain rule from the loss back to neuron j's weight:

\og{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j}} = \og{2e}\cdot\pw{v_j}\cdot f'(z_j)\cdot\bl{x}
\og{2e}: how wrong the output was at this point, and in which direction.
\pw{v_j}: how much the output listens to neuron j.
f'(z_j): was neuron j switched on for this point? ReLU's slope (§6) is 1 if on and 0 if off.
\bl{x}: the input, since w_j multiplied it.
For the bias the last factor is just 1: \og{\partial L/\partial b_j} = \og{2e}\cdot\pw{v_j}\cdot f'(z_j).
Show where those factors come from (chain rule)
\begin{aligned} z_j &= \pw{w_j}x + \pw{b_j}\\ h_j &= f(z_j)\\ \hat y &= \textstyle\sum_k \pw{v_k} h_k + \pw{c}\\[6pt] \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} &= \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}}_{2e}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial h_j}}_{v_j}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial h_j}{\partial z_j}}_{f'(z_j)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial w_j}}_{x}\end{aligned}

Each link in the chain is one simple derivative. Multiply them together. That is all backpropagation is (§10).

A neuron is only blamed for points where it was on. For every other point its gradient is exactly 0, so off neurons don't move.
Tap a white dot in the graph above to inspect its blame

Training adds up these per-point blames over all 25 points (and divides by 25), then steps every weight against the total. Points the curve misses pull on whichever hinges are switched on near them.

A neuron that is off for every data point has all-zero gradients, so it never moves again. This is a dead ReLU. It's one reason networks start from random weights: so the neurons begin switched on in different places.
A ReLU neuron is a hinge. The network's output is a sum of hinges. Training slides and tilts each hinge, pulled by the errors of the points where it's switched on. That's what "the network learned a feature" means.
§9 · The same idea in 2D: layers model

Hinges become creases

With two inputs, a ReLU neuron h = \text{ReLU}(\pw{w_1}x_1 + \pw{w_2}x_2 + \pw{b}) is a flat sheet folded along a straight crease, the line where \pw{w_1}x_1 + \pw{w_2}x_2 + \pw{b} = 0 (the same line as §5). It's 0 on one side of the crease and rises like a ramp on the other.

Below, a network with 4 such neurons (a hidden layer of 4) feeds one output neuron with a sigmoid, like §5. Its job: the circle from §7, with only x_1 and x_2 as inputs, no hand-made feature.

2 inputs → 4 ReLU neurons → 1 output · dashed = creases, yellow = boundary

Each neuron's ramp: dark where it's off, brighter the further past its crease

The same thing, written with matrices

Stack the four neurons' weights as the rows of a matrix \pw{W_1}. Then the whole layer is one line of algebra:

\begin{aligned}\mathbf h &= \text{ReLU}(\pw{W_1}\,\bl{\mathbf x} + \pw{\mathbf b_1}) &&\text{(4 numbers)}\\ z &= \pw{W_2}\,\mathbf h + \pw{b_2}, \quad p = \sigma(z) &&\text{(1 number)}\end{aligned}
\pw{W_1} is 4\times 2: row j holds neuron j's two weights. Multiplying by \bl{\mathbf x} (2\times1) gives all four weighted sums at once (4\times1). \pw{W_2} is 1\times4, the output weights v_j. ReLU is applied to each of the 4 numbers separately.

\pw{W_2}(\pw{W_1}\bl{\mathbf x} + \pw{\mathbf b_1}) + \pw{b_2} = \underbrace{(\pw{W_2W_1})}_{1\times2}\bl{\mathbf x} + \underbrace{(\pw{W_2}\pw{\mathbf b_1} + \pw{b_2})}_{\text{a number}}
which is a single neuron, so a single straight line. Ten such layers would still collapse into one.
In 2D each ReLU neuron is a crease. A layer of them is \text{ReLU}(\pw{W}\bl{\mathbf x} + \pw{\mathbf b}). Gradient descent moves the creases until the combined shape fits the data. Deep learning = layers that learn their own features, using the same loss and gradient descent as §2–§4.
§10 · Backpropagation and the training loop optimizer

Blame flows backwards

In §8 you got a hidden neuron's gradient by chaining four simple derivatives together. A deeper network just has longer chains. Backpropagation ("backprop") is the efficient way to get every weight's gradient in one go: start at the loss and walk backwards through the network, one layer at a time.

Why backwards? Every weight's chain starts with the same first link, \og{\partial L/\partial \hat y}, and neighbouring weights share most of their links. Walking backwards computes each shared piece once and hands it to the layer before. So one backward pass costs about the same as one forward pass, whether the network has 17 weights or your CNN's 10,330.

The smallest chain, by hand

One input, two weights, no activation (so the numbers stay simple), one training example with target y = 2:

\bl{x}=1.5 \;\xrightarrow{\;\times\pw{w_1}\;}\; h \;\xrightarrow{\;\times\pw{w_2}\;}\; \hat y \;\longrightarrow\; \og{L} = (\hat y - 2)^2

Notice \og{\partial L/\partial h} in step ③: it's computed once, then reused to get \og{\partial L/\partial w_1}. That reuse is the whole trick.

The general rule

Call \og{\delta_j} = \og{\partial L/\partial z_j} the blame arriving at neuron j's weighted sum. Then backprop is two lines, applied layer by layer from the output back:

\begin{aligned} \og{\delta_j} &= f'(z_j)\,\sum_k \pw{W_{kj}}\,\og{\delta_k}\\[4pt] \og{\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ji}}} &= \og{\delta_j}\cdot a_i \end{aligned}
Line 1: a neuron's blame = the blame of the neurons it feeds, sent back through the connecting weights, then gated by its own slope (0 if it was an off ReLU). Line 2: a weight's gradient = blame at its output end × the activity a_i at its input end. In §8 these were exactly \og{\delta_j} = 2e\cdot\pw{v_j}\cdot f'(z_j) and a_i = \bl{x}.
③ Backproploss.backward()
Computes \og{\partial L/\partial w} for every weight with the chain rule. Changes no weights.
④ Optimizeroptimizer.step()
Uses those gradients: w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\og{\partial L/\partial w}. Computes no gradients.

Your Phase 0 loop is exactly this, with 10,330 weights and batches of 64 images:

for x, y in train_loader:
    logits = model(x)            # ① forward
    loss = loss_fn(logits, y)    # ② loss
    optimizer.zero_grad()        #   wipe old gradients
    loss.backward()              # ③ backprop
    optimizer.step()             # ④ update

Why zero_grad()? PyTorch adds each new gradient to whatever is already stored, so the old ones must be cleared first.

Backprop = the chain rule, run backwards, giving every weight its gradient. The optimizer then steps every weight downhill. These are two separate jobs, repeated for every batch.

Problem 1: too many weights. A fully connected layer (§9's \pw{W_1}, just bigger) gives every pixel its own weight into every neuron. More weights need more data to learn well.

Problem 2: it ignores neighbours. A fully connected layer has no idea which pixels sit next to each other. Shuffle the pixels in a fixed order and, to that layer, nothing has changed. To you, the image is gone.

The fix: one small filter, reused everywhere

A convolution slides a small grid of weights, a filter \pw{K} (3 × 3 = 9 weights), across the image. At each position it does what every neuron does, a weighted sum plus a bias, but only over the 9 neighbouring pixels:

\text{out}[i,j] = \sum_{a=0}^{2}\sum_{c=0}^{2} \pw{K[a,c]}\;\bl{x[i+a,\ j+c]} + \pw{b}
The same 9 weights are used at every (i, j), called weight sharing, which fixes problem 1. Each output only looks at a pixel's neighbours, which fixes problem 2. Your conv1 has 16 filters: 16\times(9 + 1) = 160 parameters, against 784\times100 + 100 = 78{,}500 for a fully connected layer with 100 neurons. After the convolution comes ReLU: each filter's output goes through the same hinge you met in §8.

One 3 × 3 filter, the same 9 weights at every position. Edge-detecting weights shown illustrative

A CNN is the same recipe (weighted sums, ReLU hinges, a loss, backprop, an optimizer) with one change: small filters whose weights are shared across the image. Everything else on this page carries over.
