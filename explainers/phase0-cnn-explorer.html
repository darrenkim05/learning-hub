§2 · Step 1 · Load data

  
An image is just a grid of numbers

  
FashionMNIST is 70,000 small grayscale photos of clothing, each 28 × 28 pixels and each tagged with one of 10 classes (T-shirt, Trouser, … Ankle boot).

  
Pixel: one square of the image, stored as one brightness number. Label: the right answer for an image, stored as a number 0–9 (0 = T-shirt/top, 9 = Ankle boot).

ToTensor (÷ 255): squashes 0–255 into 0–1. Normalize: subtracts the dataset's average brightness (mean) and divides by its spread (std), so values are centred near 0. Background pixels become negative (red), garment pixels positive (blue). Centred numbers make training steadier.

  


  
What's a batch?

  
Batch: a handful of images processed together. The model learns from 64 at a time instead of one by one, which is faster and gives a steadier signal.

§3 · Step 2 · Define model · conv1

  
A filter slides over the image

  
A filter is a tiny 3 × 3 grid of weights. Lay it on the image, multiply each of the 9 pixels under it by the weight on top, and add the 9 results (plus one extra number, the bias). That sum becomes one pixel of the output. Slide one pixel right and repeat, 28 × 28 = 784 times.

  
Weight: a number the model learns during training. Feature map: the output grid, which is bright wherever the image matches the filter's pattern. Padding: a border of zeros around the image so the filter can sit on edge pixels too.

§4 · Step 2 · Define model · ReLU, MaxPool

  
Keep the positives, then shrink

  
ReLU ("rectified linear unit"): replace every negative number with 0 and leave positives alone. In symbols, ReLU(x) = max(0, x). It's the simple bend that lets stacked layers learn more than straight-line patterns.

  

    
Feature map from §3 illustrative

    
    

    

    

  

  
Max pooling (2×2): chop the map into 2 × 2 squares and keep only the biggest number from each. Width and height halve, the strongest signals survive, and later layers get less to process.

Why is the linear layer so big?

  

    
    
Weight sharing: a conv filter's 9 weights are reused at every one of the 784 positions, so conv1 needs only 16 × (9 + 1) = 160 numbers. The linear layer has no sharing: each of its 784 inputs connects to each of its 10 outputs with a weight of its own, so 784 × 10 + 10 = 7,850.

    

  

  







  
§6 · Step 2 · Define model · flatten, linear

  
From 16 maps to 10 scores

  
Flatten computes nothing. It takes the 16 little 7 × 7 maps that come out of block 2 and lays their numbers end to end in one row: 16 × 7 × 7 = 784.

  

    
Flatten your image's (16, 7, 7) illustrative weights 

    
    

    
Position in the row = channel × 49 + row × 7 + column (PyTorch's order).

  

  
Linear layer: for each class, multiply all 784 numbers by 784 weights, add them up, add a bias. The result is that class's logit, a raw score that can be any number. Higher means "more like this class".

§7 · Step 3 · Loss

  
How wrong was that?

  
The loss turns 10 logits plus the true label into one number that says how wrong the model was. It happens in two moves:

  
Softmax: turns logits into probabilities (positive, summing to 1). Each logit is exponentiated (elogit), then divided by the total.
Cross-entropy loss: −ln(probability given to the true class). If the model gave the right answer probability 1, the loss is 0. As that probability heads to 0, the loss shoots up.

§8 · Step 4 · Optimizer (and backprop)

  
Two different jobs

  

    
① Backproploss.backward()
For every weight, works out its gradient: "how much did you contribute to the loss?" Changes no weights.

    
② Optimizeroptimizer.step()
Reads those gradients and nudges each weight a little against its gradient (downhill). Computes no gradients.

  

  
Gradient: a weight's slope. +2.4 means "raising this weight raises the loss quickly, so lower it". Learning rate (lr): how big a step the optimizer takes. The update rule (plain SGD) is w ← w − lr × gradient.

§9 · Step 5 · Training loop

  
Loops inside loops

§10 · Step 6 · Evaluate

  
Exam on unseen questions

  
The test set is 10,000 FashionMNIST images that never appear in training. The model never got a gradient from them, so its score on them shows how well it generalizes, i.e. how it does on new clothes.

  
Overfitting: the model memorizes quirks of its training images instead of learning general patterns. Training accuracy keeps rising while accuracy on new images stalls or drops. Testing on training data would hide this, like grading a student with the exact homework questions they already saw.
